Rubrics: Define "Good" Once

When you ask AI to "make this better," the word better is doing a lot of undefined work. The model guesses what you mean by good. A rubric replaces that guess with a standard you set.

A rubric is a written description of what strong work looks like. Once you've written one, you can hand it to AI and get a consistent read on any draft, yours or the model's, from any chat or project space.

Write down what good looks like, and you can check anything against it, anywhere.

This guide covers what a rubric is in this context, how to get AI to build one with you, the two ways to put it to work, and where the same idea shows up inside more advanced AI systems.

The Idea

What a Rubric Is

A rubric here is not a school grading sheet. It's a short list of the specific things that make a piece of work strong, written plainly enough that AI can check against each one.

Think of it as a yardstick. Instead of asking "is this a good proposal," you spell out what a good proposal has: a clear problem statement, scope the client can actually approve, pricing tied to value, no jargon a buyer wouldn't use. Each line becomes something the AI, or you, can look for and judge.

Key concept

The more concrete each criterion, the more useful the grade. "Well-organized" is hard to evaluate. "Each section opens with its conclusion, then supports it" is easy. Vague criteria produce vague feedback.

That's the whole trick. A rubric forces you to say what you actually mean by quality, in terms specific enough to act on. The work of writing it is most of the value.

Getting Started

Build One by Prompting

You don't write the rubric from scratch. You prompt AI to draft it, then you refine, because you're the authority on what good means in your context.

The prompt depends on what you're grading. Tell the model what kind of work it is, who it's for, and what matters most. Then ask it to propose the criteria.

Try this

"I evaluate client status reports written for executive sponsors. Draft a rubric of six to eight criteria that separates a strong report from a weak one. For each criterion, describe in one line what 'strong' and 'weak' look like."

What comes back is a starting point, not the answer. Cut criteria that don't matter to you, sharpen the ones that do, and add anything it missed. The editing is where your judgment goes in, and it's what makes the rubric yours rather than a generic checklist.

Note

Save the finished rubric somewhere you can reuse it. The next section covers two ways to make it reusable so you're not re-pasting criteria every time.

Putting It to Work

Two Ways to Use It

Once you have a rubric you trust, there are two ways to put it to work. They take about the same effort, so pick based on how you'll use it.

🧰

A standalone grader skill

Build a skill whose only job is to evaluate work against this rubric. Once it's saved, you can grade a draft from any chat or project space without re-pasting the criteria. Good when the rubric applies to lots of different documents.

➕

Grader mode in a skill you already have

If you already have a skill that produces something, like a WBS or a status report, tell it to include a mode that checks its own output, or yours, against the rubric before handing it back. Good when grading belongs next to the work itself.

Both assume you're comfortable building a skill. If that part is new, start with Build Your First Skill in 15 Minutes, then come back and point it at your rubric.

In Practice

Rubrics Worth Building

The technique pays off anywhere "good" is fuzzy and you produce the same kind of work more than once. A few that map to how the team actually works:

  • Requirements strength: does each one say who needs it and what "done" looks like, with nothing left to interpretation?
  • Status report quality: does it lead with what the sponsor needs to know and surface risks before they become urgent?
  • Proposal strength: clear problem, scope the client can approve, pricing tied to value, language a buyer would actually use.
  • Client email: the ask up front, no buried action item, tone matched to the relationship.
  • Meeting summary: decisions and owners captured, not a transcript of who said what.
  • SOW section: deliverables specific enough that both sides know what "complete" means.
Advanced Use Case

Using Rubrics with Agents

What you've just built is the lightweight version of something more advanced AI setups do at a much larger scale.

First, what's an agent?

Most of the time you're working with AI in a back-and-forth: you prompt, it answers, you prompt again. An agent is AI set up to work through a multi-step task more on its own. It can plan, take actions, check its own results, and keep going until the job is done, with less hand-holding from you at each step.

That setup opens up a better way to grade. Instead of asking one AI to write the work and then grade itself, you can hand the grading to a separate agent whose only job is to score against the rubric. This is called an independent grader, or a grader sub-agent.

The separation is the whole point. When the same AI writes and grades, it tends to go easy on itself and miss the same things twice, the same way you can proofread your own email three times and still skip the typo. A separate grader comes to the work fresh. It didn't write the draft, so it has no stake in defending it, and its read is worth more because of that.

How it pays off

In an agent loop, the grader doesn't just hand back a score. It sends weak work back to be rewritten, then re-grades it, and repeats until the draft clears the bar you set in the rubric. You get vetted output instead of a first attempt you still have to check.

You don't need to build any of this to get value from a rubric. The point is to see where yours sits: the same idea behind your one-chat self-check is what scales up to grade-and-rewrite loops. Either way, treat the rubric as a forcing function you review, not an autopilot you trust blindly.
