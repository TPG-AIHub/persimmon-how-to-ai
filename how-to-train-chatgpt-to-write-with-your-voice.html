AI Tutorial

Why Build a Voice Skill

Right now, every time you ask ChatGPT to write something, you start from scratch. You describe your tone, paste in your preferences, cross your fingers. The output comes back sounding like a courteous stranger who studied your LinkedIn profile for thirty seconds.

A voice skill changes the dynamic. You teach ChatGPT how you write, once, and it remembers every time. No more re-explaining. No more rewriting drafts that were technically fine but sounded nothing like you.

This tutorial walks through the full build, from scoping what the skill should cover to installing and testing it. Budget about 30 minutes for the initial setup.
AI Tutorial

Where a Voice Skill Helps Most

A voice skill pays off anywhere the writing needs to sound like it came from you. Some categories benefit more than others.

Emails and Messages

This is where you'll feel the difference first. Follow-ups, check-ins, scheduling, status updates. The everyday writing where "sounding like you" matters most. Instead of rewriting ChatGPT's output to sound less robotic, it gets your tone right on the first pass.

Client-Facing Work

Reports, proposals, course descriptions. These may use a more polished register than your emails, but they still benefit from consistency. When a client reads your deliverable, it should sound like it was written by the same person who sends them email.

Social Media and External Writing

LinkedIn posts, blog posts, anything external where your personality needs to come through. If you do this kind of writing, a voice skill makes a big difference.

The Build — Step 1

Scope Your Skill

Before you collect writing samples or open ChatGPT, get clear on what the skill should and shouldn't do. Scoping up front saves you from rebuilding later.

List your registers

Think about the types of writing you do, then group them by tone. Emails and Teams messages might share one register (informal). Client status reports and deliverables might share another (formal). Start with two or three registers (or even just one core voice) and see whether you need more after testing.

What's a register?

A register is a version of your voice tuned for a specific context. You already shift registers naturally. You don't write a Slack message to your team the same way you write a project status email to a client. A voice skill can account for those shifts.

Decide who the author is

This is a question that matters more than it seems. Are you the author (emails, posts, updates) or is Persimmon the author (proposals, Insights articles, web copy)? This tutorial covers your personal voice. If you need a skill for Persimmon-authored content, that's a separate build (or a shared skill built for that purpose).

Name what's excluded

Decide what you do not want your skill to do. Exclusions prevent your skill from activating on the wrong task. For example: "Do not apply my personal voice to Persimmon marketing content."

✅ Use the skill ⏭️ Don't use the skill
Emails you're sending as yourself Proposals or deliverables written as Persimmon
Status updates in your own name Templates or form letters
Messages to colleagues or clients Research summaries or analysis
LinkedIn posts or blog drafts Persimmon marketing content
The Build — Step 2

Learn the Categories

Use these six categories to describe each register. In Step 3 you'll let ChatGPT help you fill them in — but it helps to know what you're looking for before you start.

01

Tone & Qualities

Warm or direct? Exploratory or authoritative? Casual or polished? Name the qualities with short examples of each.

02

Word Choice and Avoidance Rules

Words you use. Words you never use. Be specific: "Never say 'strategic alignment' or 'circle back.'"

03

Perspective & Pronoun Rules

When do you say "you," "we," or third person? Set rules for each. For example: "Use 'we' when pointing out mistakes rather than 'you.'" As in, "When we get busy, it's easy to miss the obvious."

04

Rhetorical Devices and Sentence Style

Parenthetical asides? Analogies? Dry humor? Short sentences mixed with long? ChatGPT will partially catch these from your samples, but you may need to clarify or refine.

05

Format Rules (Per Register)

How do you structure an email vs. a post? Paragraph length, bullet style, how you open and close. These differ by register. That's the whole point of having registers.

06

Judgment Heuristics

Rules you apply when editing your own work: "Read it aloud and check for accusatory language that could cause defensiveness." ChatGPT won't figure these out from your samples. You have to add them yourself.

Why six categories and not just "tone"

Telling ChatGPT "write in a professional but friendly tone" gives it almost nothing to work with. These six categories give it specific, concrete rules. The kind of detail that turns generic output into something that reads like something you wrote.

The Build — Step 3

Collect & Analyze Samples

Gather 3–5 full samples per register

For each register, pull 3–5 complete pieces. Full emails, entire LinkedIn posts, whole report sections. Not snippets. One sample per format risks ChatGPT treating a single instance as the template for everything.

What counts as a good sample

Writing where you were being yourself. Not a template someone gave you. Not something you copied from a past deliverable. The emails and updates where you just wrote? Those are the ones you want.

Ask ChatGPT to analyze each register

Paste one register's samples into ChatGPT and use this prompt:

"Analyze these writing samples. Describe my voice using these six categories: (1) Tone & Qualities, (2) Word Choice & Avoidance Rules, (3) Perspective & Pronoun Rules, (4) Rhetorical Devices, (5) Format Rules, (6) Judgment Heuristics."

Repeat for each register.

Edit into your voice guide

ChatGPT will get some things right, especially tone. But it will miss your avoidance rules and format conventions. And it will exaggerate. It might say you "consistently deploy dry humor as a strategic rhetorical device" when the reality is you're occasionally funny in emails.

Cut the overstatements. Add what's missing. Save the result as a single document, one section per register. That's your voice guide.

The editing step matters

Skipping the edit and feeding ChatGPT's raw analysis back to itself is a common mistake. The analysis is a starting point. Your editing pass is what makes it accurate. You know how you write better than ChatGPT does. The edit is where your actual voice gets into the document.

The Build — Step 4

Build the Skill

Upload your voice guide and prompt

Open a new ChatGPT conversation. Attach your voice guide (a .docx file is fine, no need to convert it) and paste this prompt:

"I've attached my voice guide. It describes [X] registers. Build me a ChatGPT skill that applies this voice whenever I'm writing as myself. The skill should detect or ask which register to use. ChatGPT must use this skill when [list from Step 1]. It must not activate when [list from Step 1]."

Review the draft skill

ChatGPT will generate a draft and show you a preview. Read through it. Check that your avoidance rules, pronoun logic, and format conventions survived the translation. Check that the activation and exclusion rules match what you defined in Step 1.

Read before you install

It's tempting to click Install immediately. Don't. The draft skill often drops specific rules, especially avoidance rules and judgment heuristics. A two-minute read-through now saves you from debugging a skill that "mostly works" for the next month.

Install and test

Click Install, then open a new chat and write something in each register. Your voice skill should activate automatically. If it doesn't, @-mention the skill by name.

Compare the output to what ChatGPT gives you without the skill. The difference should be obvious. If it's subtle, the skill needs more specifics in your voice guide. Go back to Step 3 and add detail to the categories that feel thin.
