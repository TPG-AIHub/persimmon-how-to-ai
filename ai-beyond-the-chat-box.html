AI Explainer

AI Beyond the Chat Box

You already know how to talk to AI, and you may even have a favorite. But as AI evolves, the model itself is becoming less important than what's being built on top of it.

The tools, products, and capabilities emerging around AI are unlocking a world of possibilities—even for non-technical people. The reasoning engine is a foundation, not the finish line.

This guide gives you a whirlwind tour of what's happening right now. Each section covers one concept, explains why it matters, and shows you what it looks like in practice. You can read straight through or click around—they're designed to stand on their own.

Think of the AI model as an engine. Everything in this guide is what turns that engine into a vehicle that actually goes somewhere.
The First Layer

How AI Takes Action

At its core, an AI model generates text. Tools are what let it do things.

When you ask AI to search the web, it's calling a tool. When it reads a PDF you uploaded, that's a tool. When it writes and runs code, generates an image, or creates a file—all tools. They're the bridge between "AI that talks" and "AI that takes action."

Most AI platforms ship with a set of built-in tools. You don't configure them—they're just there, and the AI calls them when it decides it needs to. But tools can also be built by developers and added to a platform, which is how AI gets extended to do things its creators never planned for. (More on that when we get to connectors.)

🔍

Web Search

Searches the internet for current information instead of relying only on training data.

💻

Code Execution

Writes code and runs it—for analysis, file conversion, calculations, or building things.

📄

File Reading

Reads documents, spreadsheets, and PDFs you upload, then works with their contents.

🎨

Image Generation

Creates images, diagrams, and visuals from text descriptions.

Key concept

Tools are the first layer on top of the model. Without them, AI can only write. With them, it can search, calculate, read, create, and act. Everything else in this guide builds on this foundation.

Making AI Yours

From Stranger to Colleague

Tools give AI capabilities. Context gives it judgment—the knowledge to use those capabilities the way you'd want.

Without context

AI is a smart stranger.

You say "write me a proposal" and get a generic template. You spend 45 minutes reformatting.

With context

AI is a colleague who knows how you work.

You say "write me a proposal" and it asks the right questions, uses your format, matches your voice.

This is one of the fastest-evolving areas in AI. Every major platform is building new ways to let you give AI more context. The names differ, but the pattern is the same.

Project Spaces

A workspace where you give AI a set of instructions and files that persist across conversations. Instead of re-explaining your preferences every time, the project space holds that context for you. Claude calls them Projects. ChatGPT calls them Projects. Microsoft Copilot calls them Notebooks. The concept is the same: a dedicated home for a topic or workstream where AI already knows the ground rules.

Memory

AI that remembers facts and preferences across sessions—your name, your role, your formatting preferences, the project you're working on—without you restating them every time you start a new conversation.

Skills

A reusable set of instructions that teaches AI how to do a specific task your way—without re-explaining the details each time. Think of it like training a colleague: you walk them through the process once, and from then on they just do it. Skills range from simple (format a document in our brand template) to sophisticated (stress-test my proposal for logical weaknesses).

Want to go deeper on skills?

We have a separate guide covering skill types, how to build them, and how to chain multiple skills together in a single conversation—where one skill drafts your proposal, another checks it for AI-sounding language, and a third reviews it for persuasion. Three passes, one conversation, no copy-paste.

Extending AI's Reach

AI Inside Your Apps

Everything so far happens inside a chat window. Plug-ins flip the direction—they put AI inside the apps you already use.

Instead of copying content out of PowerPoint, pasting it into a chat, getting AI's response, and pasting it back, plug-ins let AI work directly inside your document. The AI sees what you see and edits where you edit.

PowerPoint

This is likely where your team will feel plug-ins first. AI inside PowerPoint can restructure a deck from an outline, restyle slides to match a template, generate speaker notes, turn a wall of text into a visual layout, or create new slides that match the look and feel of existing ones. You stay in PowerPoint the whole time.

Try this in PowerPoint
  • "Turn slide 19 into a slide about plug-ins, matching the style of slide 18."
  • "Add speaker notes to every slide summarizing the key points."
  • "Restructure this 30-slide deck into a tighter 15-slide version."
  • "Create three new slides from this outline, using the design of slide 4."

Excel

AI in Excel can write formulas, build charts, analyze data, create pivot tables, and build financial models from raw data—all from natural language. You describe what you want; it builds the formula or chart.

Word & Outlook

In Word, AI can rewrite sections for tone, tighten verbose drafts, or apply formatting. In Outlook, it can draft replies, summarize long email threads, or pull out action items from a chain of messages.

Watch out

Plug-ins can edit your live document directly. That's what makes them fast—and why you should review every change before saving. AI is confident, not infallible.

Extending AI's Reach

AI Across Your Tools

Plug-ins work inside an app. Connectors work between apps.

Connectors let AI read from and write to other tools from your AI platform. Think of them as bridges: Claude connecting to Notion, ChatGPT reaching into Google Drive, Copilot pulling from Teams. You stay in your AI conversation while it reaches out to the tools where your work actually lives.

Examples
  • "That's a good idea for a newsletter. Add that to my idea database in Notion."
  • "Read all of my voice guidelines in Notion before you draft this."
  • "Send this outline to Canva to create a LinkedIn image."
  • "Pull up last quarter's project notes from Google Drive."

Under the hood, connectors are really just custom tools—the same concept from the Tools section, but built by developers to bridge specific platforms. The emerging standard for connecting these tools is called MCP (Model Context Protocol), which is making it easier for any tool to work with any AI platform.

Important

You're still driving each action—this isn't autonomous. But connectors require careful thought: which ones to enable, what permissions to grant, and what data ends up stored in the third-party tool.

Extending AI's Reach

Build Tools Without Code

This is the one that surprises people: you can now describe what you want built, and AI writes the code for you.

Products like Claude Code and OpenAI's Codex let you build tools, automations, and workflows by describing them in plain language. This isn't about becoming a developer. It's about the fact that "I wish I had a tool that does X" is now a solvable problem—even if you've never written a line of code.

What non-coders are building
  • A dashboard that pulls data from a spreadsheet and visualizes it
  • A custom intake form that routes submissions to the right person
  • An automation that reformats meeting notes into a standard template
  • A small web app that calculates project pricing based on your rate card
  • A script that cleans and merges messy data files from clients

The shift is significant: instead of requesting a tool from IT or buying software, you describe what you need and have a working version in minutes. It may not be production-grade, but it's often good enough—and you can iterate on it conversationally. "Make the chart bigger. Add a column for notes. Export to PDF."

Key concept

This doesn't replace professional development. But it eliminates the gap between "I need a tool" and "I have a tool"—especially for internal workflows that would never justify a formal build.

AI That Acts

AI That Does the Work

Everything above makes AI better at responding. This is where AI starts doing.

"Agentic" means AI takes multiple steps on its own—reading files, making intermediate decisions, calling tools, producing outputs—without you approving each step along the way. It's the difference between a tool that answers and an assistant that works.

Manually kicked off

You give AI a complex, multi-step task and let it figure out how to get there.

Example

"Here's my calendar screenshot and my to-do list. Build my plan for the week based on my preferences." AI reads both inputs, cross-references deadlines, identifies conflicts, matches your stated preferences, and produces a structured weekly plan. You gave one instruction; AI took a dozen steps.

Scheduled or autonomous

AI runs on a trigger or schedule without you kicking it off. Responsible agents have guardrails—permission points where a human reviews before anything goes out the door.

Example

When coaching session notes hit a database, an agent rewrites them into a participant email summary using your template, rules, and voice—then emails you the draft for review before sending.

Sub-agents

As tasks get more complex, agents can delegate to other agents. A "project update" agent might call a "data analysis" sub-agent to crunch numbers, then pass the results to a "report formatting" sub-agent. You see the final output; the coordination happened behind the scenes.

The blurring line

Here's what's worth noticing: even ordinary chat conversations are becoming agentic. When you ask Claude a question and it decides to search the web, read a file, and run some code before responding—that's agentic behavior. The AI chose which tools to use, in what order, without you specifying. The line between "chat" and "agent" is blurring by design.

A note on "agents" in the wild

You'll hear the word "agent" used loosely—especially from clients using Microsoft Copilot or ChatGPT. When a knowledge worker says they're "building agents," they're most likely creating customized AI assistants with specific instructions and knowledge—using Copilot's Agent Builder or ChatGPT's Custom GPTs. These are closer to what we'd call a skill with a persona: powerful, but not the fully autonomous, multi-step agents described above. The word is the same; the sophistication varies widely. If a client mentions agents, it's worth asking what they mean.

Key concept

If the earlier sections are attachments that make the vacuum more useful, agentic AI redesigns the vacuum entirely. Everything before this point helps AI respond better. This is where AI starts doing the work.

AI That Acts

AI on Your Desktop & Browser

The most advanced expression of agentic AI: an agent that can see your screen and use your tools.

Computer use

AI can now operate your computer—open applications, navigate interfaces, create files, and work across tools, even ones with no AI integration. Claude's version is called Cowork: a desktop agent that sees your screen, works with your files, and operates your applications the way you would.

Example

"Take this content and create a formatted Word document using our template, then save it to my desktop." The agent opens Word, applies the template, formats the content, and saves—without you touching the application.

How this differs from connectors

Connectors link to specific platforms through a built integration (Claude → Notion). Computer use works with anything on your screen—including tools that have no AI integration at all. It's the universal fallback.

Browser use

Same idea, applied to the web. AI navigates websites on your behalf—researching, filling forms, comparing options, extracting information. Instead of you doing the browsing, AI does the legwork.

Example

"Take these receipts and enter them into our expense system." The AI navigates the expense portal, clicks "new entry," fills fields, uploads files—the same steps you'd take, just faster.

Current limitation

Both computer use and browser use are in beta or early release. Capabilities are expanding fast, but proceed carefully—these tools can see anything on your screen or in your browser, including sensitive data and company assets.

🛡️

Policy Alert

Plug-ins, connectors, and code/codex tools extend AI's reach into our systems and data. That reach requires oversight.

Persimmon does not allow installing plug-ins, browser extensions, connectors, or code/codex tools without prior approval. This includes AI integrations inside apps like PowerPoint and Excel, connections between AI platforms and tools like Notion or Teams, and any code-generating tools like Claude Code or Codex.

Approval includes a security review to ensure our data and systems remain protected.

If you're interested in using any of these tools, talk to Dianne or a member of the ELT before getting started. We want to enable these capabilities where they make sense—we just need to do it safely.
🛡️

Policy Alert

Agentic AI, computer use, and browser use are powerful—but they introduce a different level of risk when connected to Persimmon systems.

These tools can see your screen, access your browser, and take actions across applications—including applications that contain company data, client information, and sensitive assets. By default, Persimmon does not allow agentic AI, computer use, or browser use tools.

Persimmon may grant exceptions with appropriate guardrails, based on your role and the specific use case.

Talk to Dianne or a member of the ELT before experimenting with any of these tools on company systems or data. We're actively evaluating where these capabilities can be used responsibly.
